# Impact statement (draft v2)

## Positive impacts
Students in rural JHS classrooms could revise Integrated Science offline, at their own pace, with
explanations matched to the Ghanaian curriculum, from shared phones or old laptops connected to a
low-cost Raspberry Pi. Teachers could save preparation time and focus on the students who need
the most help. Because the model is small, fine-tuned once and runs on local hardware, it avoids
ongoing data-centre costs and works where internet and power are unreliable.

## Negative impacts / risks
- **Wrong answers:** a confident but incorrect card could teach a misconception before an exam.
- **Over-reliance:** students or schools might treat the tool as a substitute for teachers or
  textbooks, or use it as a shortcut instead of understanding.
- **Language and access:** English-only answers may leave behind students who learn best in Twi
  or other local languages; schools without devices or power still cannot use it.
- **Bias in coverage:** topics I write more cards for will be answered better, so weaker topics
  may be quietly underserved.
- **Environment:** a small footprint, but devices and the Pi eventually become e-waste.

## Values
Fairness (rural and urban students deserve equally good revision support), dignity and autonomy
(teachers stay in control of content; the tool supports rather than replaces them), and
well-being (reducing exam stress without spreading misinformation). There is a tension between
efficiency (automated cards at scale) and accuracy (human review takes time).

## Scale
In one classroom, a teacher can spot and correct errors. At scale (many schools, many subjects),
any systematic error or gap would spread widely and be harder to catch, so scaling up would
require teacher review panels, error reporting and alignment with the Ghana Education Service.

## Uncertainty
It is unclear how students will actually use the cards (revision vs copying), how accurate the
model will be on topics outside the training data, and how well a Pi 4 performs in a real
classroom. A small pilot with one teacher and class, and feedback from students and parents, would
reduce these uncertainties.
